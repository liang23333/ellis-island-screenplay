# Italian Immigrant Culture, Labor & Daily Life in New York (1905)

## The Mezzogiorno Exodus

Between 1880 and 1914, over 4 million Italians immigrated to the United States; over 80% originated from the **Mezzogiorno** (the southern regions of Italy: Calabria, Campania, Sicily, Basilicata, and Abruzzo-Molise).

### Root Drivers of Emigration (1900–1905)
- **Crushing Agricultural Poverty:** Latifundia (massive aristocratic estates) leased tiny, exhausted land plots to *contadini* (peasant sharecroppers) under feudal contracts.
- **Ecological Disasters:** The phylloxera epidemic destroyed Southern Italian vineyards; severe malaria plagued the lowland river valleys; deforestation caused disastrous soil erosion.
- **State Oppression:** Post-unification (*Risorgimento*) Rome imposed heavy grain and salt taxes (*tassa sul macinato*) on the South while investing industrial capital into the North.
- **The "Birds of Passage" (*Ritornati*):** Roughly 30% to 50% of Italian male immigrants in this era initially planned to work for 2 to 5 years, save every penny in gold dollars, and return home to buy land (*la terra*).

## Language, Regionalism & *Campanilismo*

There was no unified "Italian" culture or language among the immigrants of 1905:
- **Campanilismo:** Fierce loyalty to one's own village or bell tower (*campanile*). A Calabrian peasant from Catanzaro had zero cultural kinship with a Venetian or Genoese, and viewed a Sicilian with deep suspicion.
- **Dialect Barriers:** Immigrants spoke regional dialects (*dialetti*) that were distinct, mutually unintelligible languages. Standard Italian (*lingua toscana*) was only understood by educated elites. On the docks, miscommunication between southern peasants and northern-educated Italian consular clerks or interpreters was rampant.
- **Street-by-Street Enclaves in Manhattan's Little Italy:**
  - **Mulberry Street (Canal to Broome):** Dominantly Neapolitans and Campanians.
  - **Elizabeth Street:** Heavily Sicilian (particularly from Palermo and Sciacca).
  - **Mott Street:** Calabrians and Basilicatans.
  - **Baxter Street & Sullivan Street:** Genoese and Northern Italians (who arrived earlier in the 1870s and distanced themselves from the "darker" southerners).

## The Padrone System & Labor Realities (1905)

Because newly arrived *contadini* spoke no English, had no bank accounts, and lacked marketable industrial trades, they fell into the hands of the **Padrone**:

### How the Padrone Operated
- **The Middleman:** The *padrone* (boss) was usually an earlier, established immigrant who ran a saloon, grocery store, or private travel agency on Mulberry Street.
- **Contract Labor Broker:** American railroad companies, municipal contractors, and mining firms contracted with padroni to deliver gangs of 50 to 500 cheap laborers.
- **System of Exploitation:**
  - The padrone advanced the immigrant money for steamship passage or inland train fares at exorbitant interest (up to 20–30%).
  - He deducted fees for board, grog, tools, and shanty housing.
  - Immigrants were paid $1.25 to $1.75 for a backbreaking 10-hour day of physical labor (pick-and-shovel excavation).
- **Major 1905 NYC Work Sites for Italians:**
  - **The IRT Subway Expansion:** Following the opening of the first NYC subway line in October 1904, thousands of Italian pick-and-shovel men dug trenches through Manhattan bedrock under dangerous conditions.
  - **The Pennsylvania Station Excavation:** Massive excavation began in 1904–1905 across 8 city blocks on Manhattan's West Side.
  - **The New Croton Dam / Water Aqueduct System:** Heavy labor in northern Westchester County.
  - **Rag-picking and Street Sweeping:** The NYC Department of Street Cleaning ("White Wings") employed Italian street scavengers and ash haulers.

## Tenement Life: Inside Mulberry Bend & Lower East Side

### Architecture of the "Dumbbell" Tenement
- Built under the Old Tenement House Act of 1879, dumbbell tenements were 5 to 6 stories high, 25 feet wide by 100 feet deep.
- **Layout:** Four apartments per floor, each with only 3 or 4 rooms:
  - Parlor (10x12 ft) facing the street or rear yard.
  - Kitchen with cast-iron coal stove and cold-water sink.
  - 1 or 2 interior bedrooms (7x8 ft) with windows opening onto a narrow, stagnant 28-inch-wide airshaft.
- **The Airshaft:** A notorious fire trap and health hazard, usually filled with foul air, garbage thrown from upper floors, and rotting debris.
- **Overcrowding:** A 3-room apartment designed for 4 people routinely housed 10 to 14 individuals, as families took in single male *boarders* (*paesani*) who slept in shifts on straw mattresses on the floor to cover the $12–$16 monthly rent.

### Domestic Life & Women's Piecework
- Italian women rarely worked outside the home in factories due to strict southern cultural norms of family honor (*la famiglia*).
- **Home Piecework:** Women and young children transformed tenement kitchens into sweatshops:
  - Bending wire and pasting silk petals for artificial flowers (paid 8–10 cents per gross).
  - Finishing coats and men's trousers for the garment district (*bastitura*).
  - Rolling cigars and shelling nuts.
- **Cooking & Diet:**
  - Staples: Polenta, dried beans (*fagioli*), stale bread soaked in chicory coffee, salted cod (*baccalà*), lard, garlic, and wild greens scavenged from city outskirts.
  - American social workers and charity visitors in 1905 criticized Italian diets as "insalubrious" and "overly pungent," attempting to force assimilation through bland American foods.

## Social Solidarity & Hostility

### Mutual Aid & Religion
- **Società di Mutuo Soccorso:** Over 200 mutual aid societies existed in Little Italy by 1905, organized strictly by native town (e.g., *Società San Fratello*, *Società San Vito*). Members paid 25 to 50 cents a month to guarantee sick benefits ($5/week) and a dignified funeral with a brass band.
- **The Catholic Church Tension:** Irish-American clergy dominated the New York Archdiocese (under Archbishop John Farley). Irish priests often relegated Italians to dark church basements, considering their street festivals, fireworks, and statue-carrying processions (*feste*) to be semi-pagan idolatry. In response, Italian parishes (such as the Scalabrini Fathers at Church of the Transfiguration and Most Holy Redeemer) were established.

### Nativist Hostility & Racialization
- In 1905, the American press and scientific racists (including Dillingham Commission theorists) classified Southern Italians as racially distinct from Northern Europeans—frequently labeling them as "swarthy," prone to passions and violent crime, and mentally inferior.
- Slurs such as "dago," "guinea," and "guappo" were widespread in newspaper headlines.
- The emergent sensationalist press stoked fear of the **"Black Hand" (*La Mano Nera*)**—extortion rings run by Italian criminals targeting wealthy Italian grocers and merchants with threatening letters stamped with skulls and daggers.
