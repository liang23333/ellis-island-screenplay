# Historical Climate & Weather Archive: New York City (January 1905)

## Meteorological Summary: The Brutal Winter of 1904–1905

The winter of 1904–1905 was recorded by the United States Weather Bureau (Central Park Observatory) as one of the most relentlessly severe winters of the early 20th century, characterized by back-to-back blizzards, Arctic cold waves, and treacherous maritime conditions in New York Harbor.

### Significant Storm Events of January 1905

1. **The Great Gale & Blizzard of January 3–5, 1905:**
   - **Precipitation:** 9.0 to 10.5 inches of dense snowfall across the New York metropolitan area.
   - **Winds:** Sustained north-northwest gales of 45–55 mph, with gusts exceeding 60 mph recorded in the harbor.
   - **Impact:** Described by contemporary newspapers (*Rocky Mountain News*, *The New York Times*) as the worst storm since the historic Blizzard of 1888. Seven men froze to death in Manhattan streets; harbor ferry services were halted due to blinding whiteout conditions; snowdrifts reached 6 to 8 feet against tenement stoops.

2. **The Mid-Month Arctic Freeze (January 12–18, 1905):**
   - **Temperatures:** Persistent Arctic air mass settled over the Northeast. Daily maximum temperatures hovered between 22°F and 28°F (-5°C to -2°C), while overnight lows routinely plummeted to 8°F to 14°F (-13°C to -10°C).
   - **Harbor Conditions:** Chunks of pack ice drifted down the Hudson and East Rivers into Upper New York Bay. Salt spray instantly froze upon striking ship hulls, coating ropes, companionways, and luggage in thick rime glaze.

3. **The Blizzard of January 24–26, 1905:**
   - **Precipitation:** 12 to 14 inches of wind-whipped snow.
   - **Impact:** Completely paralyzed city rail and horse-car transit. Food supply logistics collapsed for 48 hours; over 10 deaths from hypothermia were reported across the five boroughs.

## Sensory and Environmental Details for the Screenplay

### On Board the Steerage Decks & Transfer Barges
- **Freezing Spray:** When the steamship idles at quarantine and passengers are loaded onto transfer barges (*lighters*), the harbor chop kicks up a freezing mist that turns wool coats stiff with frost.
- **Inadequate Clothing:** Immigrants from Calabria, Sicily, or Campania often wear clothing suited to a Mediterranean climate—coarse fustian jackets, unlined woolen shawls (*scialli*), thin cotton shirts, and worn leather or wooden-soled work boots. Many have no gloves, wrapping their hands in rags or tucking them into their sleeves.
- **Harbor Sounds:** The groaning of wood pilings against ferry hulls, the screech of sheet ice scraping metal hulls, the piercing blast of steam whistles muffled by freezing fog.

### Inside the Ellis Island Complex
- **Thermal Disparity:** The immense volume of the Registry Room (Great Hall) was heated by cast-iron steam radiators along the perimeter. Close to the radiators, the heat was stifling and smelled of damp wool, sweat, and carbolic acid; in the center of the hall, drafts from open doors created icy currents.
- **Condensation:** Warm, moist breath from 3,000–5,000 immigrants condensed on the cold high windows, weeping down the masonry walls.

### In the Tenements of Lower Manhattan (Mulberry Bend, Hester St)
- **The Fuel Crisis:** Tenements were unheated except for small cast-iron coal/wood cookstoves in the kitchen. In January 1905, poor families spent 15 to 25 cents a bucket for bituminous coal from basement peddlers, or sent children to pick coal chunks off railroad tracks and docks.
- **Frozen Plumbing:** Hallway water faucets and exterior privy water boxes routinely froze solid in January. Tenants had to melt snow on stoves for wash water or carry buckets from street hydrants wrapped in horse manure to prevent freezing.
- **Air Quality:** Pungent blend of sulfurous coal smoke, kerosene lamp oil, damp laundry hung across interior airshafts, fried garlic, cabbage, and horse manure steaming from frozen street gutters.
